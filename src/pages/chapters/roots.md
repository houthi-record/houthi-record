<h2><span class="label">I · 1962 to 1990</span>After the imamate</h2>

A Zaydi imamate governed much of Yemen for nearly a thousand years, until the revolution of 1962 replaced it with a republic.<sup><a href="#s1">1</a>, <a href="#s2">2</a></sup> Saada, in the far north, was its historic heartland.<sup><a href="#s3">3</a></sup>

Under the republic, Zaydis lost the privileges that the imamate had given them. Salafi teaching spread into Zaydi areas, Saada among them.<sup><a href="#s2">2</a>, <a href="#s3">3</a></sup>

<h2><span class="label">II · 1992</span>The Believing Youth</h2>

In 1992 Zaydi scholars in Saada, among them Mohammed Azzan, founded the Believing Youth (*al-Shabab al-Mu'min*).<sup><a href="#s2">2</a>, <a href="#s3">3</a>, <a href="#s4">4</a></sup> It ran religious summer camps for boys and young men across the north.<sup><a href="#s2">2</a>, <a href="#s5">5</a></sup>

President Ali Abdullah Saleh's government tolerated the network and supported it, as a counterweight to the growth of Salafism.<sup><a href="#s1">1</a>, <a href="#s2">2</a></sup>

<h2><span class="label">III · 1993 to 1999</span>Hussein al-Houthi</h2>

Hussein Badr al-Din al-Houthi was the son of Badr al-Din al-Houthi, a senior Zaydi scholar in Saada. From 1993 to 1997 he sat in parliament for al-Haqq, a Zaydi party.<sup><a href="#s2">2</a>, <a href="#s3">3</a></sup>

In the late 1990s he became the leading figure in the Believing Youth.<sup><a href="#s4">4</a>, <a href="#s6">6</a></sup> He admired Iran's 1979 revolution and its leader, Ruhollah Khomeini. His views divided the network's founders, and not all of them followed him.<sup><a href="#s2">2</a>, <a href="#s4">4</a></sup>

<h2><span class="label">IV · 2000 to 2004</span>The lectures and the slogan</h2>

Hussein al-Houthi taught through lectures, which his followers recorded and transcribed. The movement publishes them today on its official media site, under his name.<sup><a href="#s9">9</a></sup>

By 2002 he had given his followers a slogan, the *sarkha* ("the scream").<sup><a href="#s2">2</a>, <a href="#s5">5</a>, <a href="#s6">6</a></sup>

<div class="both" role="note" aria-label="The slogan">
  <div lang="ar" dir="rtl"><span class="label">الصرخة</span><p>الله أكبر، الموت لأمريكا، الموت لإسرائيل، اللعنة على اليهود، النصر للإسلام</p></div>
  <div><span class="label">The sarkha</span><p>"God is the greatest. Death to America. Death to Israel. A curse upon the Jews. Victory to Islam."</p><p class="small">Translation: The Houthi Record</p></div>
</div>

His young followers shouted it in mosques after Friday prayers, in Saada and then in Sanaa. They were arrested, released, and came back to chant again.<sup><a href="#s1">1</a>, <a href="#s7">7</a></sup> The protests in Sanaa also attacked Saleh's counterterrorism cooperation with the United States.<sup><a href="#s1">1</a>, <a href="#s5">5</a></sup> By June 2004, hundreds of them were detained in Sanaa.<sup><a href="#s1">1</a>, <a href="#s2">2</a></sup>

<h2><span class="label">V · June 2004</span>The arrest order</h2>

In June 2004, after a failed attempt at reconciliation, the government sent forces to arrest Hussein al-Houthi in Saada.<sup><a href="#s1">1</a>, <a href="#s8">8</a></sup> It charged him with "harming Yemen's stability and interests" and accused him of seeking to restore the imamate.<sup><a href="#s1">1</a>, <a href="#s7">7</a></sup> His followers met the troops with arms. Chapter 02 begins there.
